Rigid versus alive

The same drawing of the same ship. The left one moves like a sticker sliding over paper. The right one moves like something drawn.

On top of that, the first film broke most of Thomas and Johnston's principles from The Illusion of Life: things moved in straight lines with one easing curve, every stagger was evenly spaced, nothing anticipated, overshot or trailed, and rigid shapes slid instead of bending.

What each switch is

  • On twos. Time is quantized to 12 drawings a second. Each drawing is held, so motion has a slight stutter that the eye reads as hand-made. Smoothness is not the goal; character is.
  • Line boil. Every seeded wobble, grain and halftone jitter re-rolls on each drawing, and the paper shifts a pixel. Many hand-drawn films boil; measured, dew does not, so it starts off here. Try it on and decide if it suits the piece.
  • Rides the waves. One wave function draws the sea lines and also lifts and tilts the ship (heave and pitch), so cause and effect agree. A sine bob unconnected to the water is the sticker problem.
  • Follow-through. The sail trails the hull's pitch by a third of a second and billows; the pennant's segments each follow the one before, late. Loose things lag; that lag is what says "cloth".
  • Oar timing. A real stroke is a quick pull with the blade in the water and a slow, lifted recovery, plus a splash at the catch. Symmetric sine swinging is a metronome.
  • Camera breath. A few pixels of slow drift, turn and zoom from noise. Nobody notices it until it stops.

The lines that matter

// On twos: hold each drawing for two frames of 24 (one of 12)
const t = Math.floor(T * 12) / 12;
setBoil(Math.floor(T * 12));            // re-roll every wobble per drawing

// One cause for both: the sea lines and the ship come from the same wave
ctx.translate(cx, wl + heave(t)); ctx.rotate(pitch(t));

// Follow-through: the sail answers the hull a third of a second late
sail.lag = (pitch(t - 0.35) - pitch(t)) * 2.4;
