Motion Studio / Drill 10

Procedural painting

A landscape from a number. Noise draws the ridges, distance washes them toward the sky, and a brush paints them in from the back.

What this teaches

  • A ridge is 1D noise. Sample noise along x, add two or three octaves at double frequency and half amplitude, and you have a skyline. Folding the noise (1 - |n|, ridged noise) gives sharp peaks for distant ranges; plain noise gives rounded foothills up close.
  • Depth is mostly color. Atmospheric perspective: each layer is mixed toward the sky color by its distance, and farther ranges are lower, smoother and more compressed. Mix in a perceptual space (OKLab here) so the steps look even.
  • Paint back to front, in time as well as order. The painter's algorithm says draw far things first. Staggered tweens with the same duration and easing keep the back layer's brush ahead of the front one at every column, and a column also waits until the layer behind it is down there, so nothing is ever painted under something nearer.
  • Seed everything, and hash per stroke. One integer drives the noise, and each stroke gets its own generator seeded from a hash of seed, layer and column. A shared stream would hand out numbers in whatever order frames happen to paint columns; a hash makes the picture independent of frame rate, so a repaint after a resize or theme change matches and a landscape can be saved as a number.
  • A timeline you drive yourself. The GSAP timeline is paused and advanced from our own frame loop, so it stops when the figure scrolls away and a finished painting costs nothing.

What gogh does

Reading the source of gogh (about 540 lines of jQuery-era JavaScript, last commit 2016) shows a different engine from this one. It uses no noise at all. Every element is a set of brush particles: each frame a particle stamps one circle and moves on, and because the canvas is never cleared, the path of the stamp becomes the stroke. A shadowBlur and shadowOffset on each stamp fakes the soft wet edge of a bristle.

The sky is about 66 particles running at 45 degrees on a rotated context, each at its own speed so stroke ends are ragged; the ground is 25 horizontal strokes. Each of one to four mountains is an isosceles triangle built from a row of particles that climb at the slope angle and stop at a line tracing the peak. Color comes from fuzzColor, which adds a random amount to the RGB channels every frame once a stroke passes a height: a random walk biased toward white that reads as sunset or snow. The tree is recursive: branches shrink about one percent a frame, split more often the farther they travel, and grow clipped-arc leaves after the second generation. Elements are drawn in groups (sky and ground, then each mountain, then the tree), and a group starts only when every particle in the one before has finished, which is the painter's algorithm played out in time.

Two small things in the code: the mountain loop calls randInt(1, 4) in its condition, so the bound is re-rolled every pass: two mountains come up most often and four only about one time in eleven, not one in four; and with plain Math.random there is no way to get a landscape back. This drill keeps gogh's best idea (strokes as the trace of a moving brush, revealed back to front) and swaps triangles for noise ridges, adds a seed, and draws the palette from the page tokens.

The lines that matter

// one ridge: fractal 1D noise, ridged far away, smooth up close
for (let o = 0, f = freq, a = 1; o < 3; o++, f *= 2, a *= 0.5) {
  const n = noise2D(u * f, layerId);
  h += a * lerp((1 - Math.abs(n)) ** 2, n * 0.5 + 0.5, nearness);
}

// atmospheric perspective: farther layers lean toward the sky token
layer.color = mixOklab(near, token('--bg'), haze * (1 - nearness) * 0.85);

// reveal: same duration, staggered starts, so the back always leads
tl.to(layers, { progress: 1, duration: 2.2, ease: 'power1.inOut', stagger: 0.55 });
